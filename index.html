Where these numbers come from
Body fat · US Navy girth method
Estimated from body measurements, not measured directly. Male: 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077 × log₁₀(waist − neck) + 0.15456 × log₁₀(height)) − 450. Female: 495 ÷ (1.29579 − 0.35004 × log₁₀(waist + hips − neck) + 0.22100 × log₁₀(height)) − 450. All girths in centimetres. Published by Hodgdon and Beckett (1984) for the US Navy. Typical agreement with a DEXA scan is around ±3–4 percentage points, and it is more reliable for tracking change over time than for any single reading. Nothing is shown until every measurement the formula needs has been logged.
Starting energy estimate · Mifflin-St Jeor
Resting rate = 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age, then +5 for men or −161 for women. Multiplied by an activity factor of 1.2 to 1.9 depending on how much you train. Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is the equation most dietetic practice uses, and is a population average: individual resting rates vary by roughly ±10% around it, more if you are very lean, very heavy, or older.
Adaptive maintenance · measured, not predicted
Once there are about two weeks of data, the app stops predicting and measures. It fits a straight line through your weigh-ins to get a trend in kg per day, and compares it with your average logged intake over the same span: maintenance = average intake − (trend × 7,700). The 7,700 kcal per kg figure is the standard approximation for body tissue. It is only an approximation: early weight change is largely water and glycogen, which carry almost no energy, so short windows overstate or understate badly. That is why 14 days is the minimum and 21–28 is preferred, and why the app flags a window it does not trust. Accuracy also depends entirely on your intake logging being honest and complete.
Daily target · your goal rate
Target = maintenance + (goal kg per week × 7,700 ÷ 7). A goal of −0.5 kg per week is a deficit of about 550 kcal a day. The app will not suggest below 1,500 kcal for men or 1,200 for women, whatever the arithmetic produces, because eating adequately below that is difficult without supervision.
Protein floor · bodyweight or lean mass
Around 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight is what the research supports for people training regularly, the higher end while losing weight.

Lean mass is the better basis, and the app uses it whenever it can. Fat tissue carries no protein requirement, so scaling by total bodyweight overstates the need for anyone carrying more of it — at 120 kg and 33% fat, bodyweight × 2.2 asks for 260 g while lean mass × 2.6 asks for 210 g, and 210 g is the honest number. Lean mass is worked back from your weight and body-fat estimate: lean = weight × (1 − fat%).

The catch is that the body-fat figure is itself an estimate, so lean mass inherits its ±3–4 point error — about ±8 g of protein, which is smaller than the difference between the two bases. So: lean mass when body fat is known, bodyweight when it is not, and the app always shows which one it used. The two converge as you get leaner; the choice matters most at higher body fat.
Next-session suggestions · patient double progression
Each movement can carry a rep range: pick Strength, Size or Endurance for the conventional bands, or set your own. The coach reads your last session of that movement at its heaviest load, so warm-up sets never count.

Reps first, then load, and one target for every set. At the same weight it asks for one more rep than your weakest set managed, on every set, within the range: last time 9, 8, 9 means at least 9 on all three next time. Straight sets at one weight are prescribed as one number, and the weakest set is exactly what stands between you and the next weight. A set that beats the target is ahead of it, not asked for less. Once every working set reaches the top in two sessions running, it adds the weight step and the target starts again from the bottom. One clean session earns a second go at the same weight rather than a heavier bar: one good day is as often a good night's sleep as real progress.

A set below the bottom of the range keeps the weight and makes the bottom the target. Holds climb five seconds at a time. A step of zero means reps only, and at the top it suggests raising the range.

The suggestions are placeholders. What you type is what is recorded, and the advice itself is never stored: it is read off your history every time, so correcting a past session changes it.
Records · estimated one-rep max
Records are read off your history every time and never stored, so a corrected or deleted day cannot leave a stale best behind. Loaded lifts keep the heaviest set and the best estimated one-rep max; bodyweight lifts keep the most reps and the heaviest added weight; holds keep the longest.

Estimated one-rep max uses Epley: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30), and only for sets of 1 to 12 reps. Past about twelve the formula drifts badly, so a set of twenty says little about a single: endurance-range sets still count towards the heaviest set, just not towards the estimate. A single counts as its own weight.

A first session sets no records, because there is nothing yet to beat.
Age and activity
Age is entered directly rather than as a year of birth, so there is no guessing whether a birthday has passed. It is stored with the date you set it and advances on its own, so the estimate does not quietly go stale — correct it any time.

Activity multipliers run 1.2 to 1.9. Every app describes these as "1–3 sessions a week" without ever saying what a session is, so this one gives hours of actual working time and judges effort by whether you can still talk — full conversation is easy, short sentences is moderate, barely a word is hard. Count your job and daily life too, not just the gym. If you are between two levels take the lower one: overshooting here is the commonest mistake, and it only affects the starting estimate anyway.
These are estimates for tracking your own training, not medical or dietary advice. Every formula here is a population average applied to one person, and every one of them can be wrong for you.
